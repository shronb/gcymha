OUR BEGINNING

Why I Founded
GCYMHA.

Written January 18, 2024

A personal journey through mental health, culture, immigration, and the experiences that shaped a commitment to supporting Chinese and Asian youth.

FOUNDER'S NOTE

Mental health support can change the course of a young person's life. GCYMHA began from a desire to make that support, understanding, and education more accessible.

I am the founder of the Global Chinese Youth Mental Health Association, a registered 501(c)(3) nonprofit organization. I would like to share, briefly, the journey that led me into the field of psychology and ultimately inspired the creation of GCYMHA.

Like many people who become deeply invested in mental health, my interest in clinical psychology began with a profoundly personal experience. For eight years, severe Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (OCD) affected nearly every part of my life.

A turning point came during my freshman year of college, when I was diagnosed with Generalized Anxiety Disorder, Complex Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder, and Trichotillomania (TTM), or hair-pulling disorder.

I vividly remember the relief that came with finally having names for what I had been experiencing — along with the regret that I had not sought help before my symptoms had progressed so far.

Growing up around stigma

Looking back for the experiences that may have contributed to my distress, I repeatedly encountered the stigma surrounding mental health within my family.

At ten years old, I tried to explain to my parents that I might be experiencing mental health difficulties. My concerns were not taken seriously. Instead, my hair-pulling behavior was described as “problematic,” “impolite,” and “inappropriate.”

At times, the suggested response was that I should leave school and live in a psychiatric hospital. Rather than helping me understand what I was experiencing, those conversations intensified the guilt and fear I already associated with thoughts and behaviors that seemed “abnormal.”

Immigration, responsibility, and isolation

After moving to the United States at age twelve, I became the primary English speaker in my family. Much of the responsibility for navigating communications outside the home fell to me.

I was balancing family responsibilities, schoolwork, extracurricular commitments, and my own personal life, often while sacrificing sleep and basic needs.

Growing up as an Asian girl and a 1.5-generation immigrant in a multicultural environment brought another form of invisibility. I often felt that people perceived me as what I have come to describe as an “NPC figure” — someone expected to perform, achieve, remain calm, and care for others without being recognized as a person with emotional needs of her own.

Academic accomplishments, care for others, and a calm temperament were frequently treated as expectations rather than achievements. The combination of limited social support, cultural stigma, and a lack of acknowledgment of my emotional needs contributed to the worsening of my stress and mental health symptoms.

The absence of visible distress does not mean that a young person is not struggling.

Discovering what support can do

Despite these challenges, I was admitted to a liberal arts college at age fifteen — an achievement that aligned closely with my parents' definition of success.

Within months, however, my understanding of success began to change.

Compassionate professors and dedicated mental health professionals offered me something I had rarely experienced before: understanding without judgment.

They encouraged me to seek psychotherapy and psychiatric care and helped me secure academic accommodations for mental health disabilities. Through these experiences, I began to understand how profoundly community care can affect a person's ability to recover, participate, and grow.

Navigating this system as a sixteen-year-old minor also exposed me to many barriers. It became increasingly clear how difficult it can be for young people to identify, access, and navigate appropriate mental health resources.

Why GCYMHA exists

My experiences also made me increasingly aware of broader disparities: unequal access to mental health care among ethnic minorities, culturally specific expressions of distress, cultural-bound syndromes, and the frequent invisibility of Asian voices within Western conversations about mental health.

These experiences led me to decide that I wanted to dedicate my work to helping Asian youth.

THE BEGINNING OF GCYMHA

Turning personal experience into community action.

I established the Global Chinese Youth Mental Health Association out of a commitment to address the mental health challenges faced by young people within Chinese communities.

Through advocacy, education, community engagement, and support, we aim to bridge gaps in access and understanding, reduce stigma, and create spaces where young people can find recognition, acceptance, and reliable resources for their mental well-being.

GCYMHA grew from one person's experience, but its purpose extends far beyond one story.

My hope is that young people will not have to wait years to understand what they are experiencing, feel ashamed of asking for help, or believe that their struggles make them less deserving of recognition, opportunity, or care.
